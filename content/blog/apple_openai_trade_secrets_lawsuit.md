On July 10, Apple filed a trade secrets lawsuit against OpenAI in the Northern District of California. A week later, on July 17, it sent formal preservation letters to roughly 40 more former Apple employees now working at OpenAI, telling them to hold onto every relevant document, email, and file. Apple's own filing calls what it's found so far the "tip of the iceberg."

This isn't a vague IP spat over a chatbot copying a UI pattern. It's a detailed complaint naming two people, describing a downloaded laptop's worth of manufacturing documents, and accusing OpenAI's hardware team of coaching new hires on how to avoid getting caught. The target is OpenAI's unreleased consumer hardware device, the one being built with Jony Ive's old design studio.

{{% tldr %}}
1. Apple sued OpenAI and its io Products subsidiary on July 10, 2026, in federal court in Northern California, alleging trade secret theft tied to unreleased AI hardware.
2. Two named individual defendants: Tang Tan, OpenAI's Chief Hardware Officer and Apple's former VP of product design, and Chang Liu, a former senior Apple systems electrical engineer.
3. The complaint alleges Liu kept an Apple-issued laptop after leaving, downloaded a 1,000+ page compilation of manufacturing documents, and exploited an access bug to reach internal network storage.
4. It also alleges Tan directed job candidates still employed at Apple to bring "actual parts" to OpenAI interviews for show-and-tell sessions, and that OpenAI misled an Apple supplier to extract confidential manufacturing details.
5. On July 17, Apple widened the fight, sending preservation letters to about 40 more former employees; more than 400 ex-Apple staff currently work at OpenAI.
6. Apple wants an injunction blocking OpenAI's use of the alleged trade secrets, plus damages, a reasonable royalty, and attorneys' fees. No dollar figure is specified yet.

## What Apple actually filed

The complaint targets OpenAI, its hardware subsidiary io Products, and two named individuals. <a href="https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/apple-OpenAI-complaint.pdf" target="_blank">Filed in the Northern District of California</a> on July 10, it alleges a coordinated effort, not a one-off leak, to obtain Apple's confidential hardware engineering, manufacturing, and supply-chain information.

io Products is the design studio Jony Ive, Apple's former chief design officer, started in 2024 with a handful of other ex-Apple leaders, including Tang Tan. OpenAI acquired it in an all-stock deal reported at roughly $6.4-6.5 billion. Notably, Ive himself is not named as a defendant, even though the firm he co-founded is.

The two people who are named:

- **Tang Tan**, who spent years as Apple's VP of product design running iPhone and Apple Watch industrial design, left for io/OpenAI in early 2024 and is now OpenAI's Chief Hardware Officer.
- **Chang Liu**, a former senior systems electrical engineer at Apple who joined OpenAI this year.

Apple says both were central to a scheme to extract information that would help OpenAI's hardware group, which is racing to ship a screenless, pocket-sized AI device reportedly aimed at the second half of 2026.

## The allegations that read like a heist movie

The parts of the complaint getting passed around aren't the legal boilerplate, they're the specific factual claims. A few, as reported by <a href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/13/the-wildest-allegations-in-apples-trade-secrets-lawsuit-against-openai/" target="_blank">TechCrunch</a> and <a href="https://fortune.com/2026/07/13/apple-lawsuit-against-openai-stolen-trade-secrets-wildest-claims/" target="_blank">Fortune</a>:

- **The laptop.** Apple alleges Liu kept his Apple-issued laptop after resigning and used it to download a compilation of technical files running over a thousand pages, including detailed documentation for the circuit boards used in Apple hardware.
- **The access bug.** Apple says Liu exploited an authentication flaw to reach internal network storage he shouldn't have had access to, and joked about it in a message to a colleague: "LOL, I found out I can access the [network storage], so funny."
- **Bring your own hardware.** The complaint claims Tan directed job candidates who were still employed at Apple to bring "actual parts" to OpenAI interviews for what internal messages call "show and tell" sessions, letting OpenAI's team extract more confidential detail than a resume conversation would.
- **The supplier angle.** Apple alleges OpenAI approached one of Apple's manufacturing partners using internal terminology "that only Apple insiders would know to ask," and separately misled another partner into believing it had Apple's blessing to use a confidential metal-finishing technique.
- **Coaching on evasion.** Apple alleges OpenAI advised departing employees on how to sidestep Apple's exit security procedures specifically to reduce the odds the alleged theft would be detected.

None of this is proven. It's Apple's version of events in a complaint, and OpenAI disputes it. But it's a level of specificity, named techniques, quoted messages, described interview procedures, that's unusual for a trade secrets filing this early.

## OpenAI's response, and a wrinkle in the filing

OpenAI's public statement has been short: "We have no interest in other companies' trade secrets. We remain focused on building innovative technology that empowers people everywhere."

There's also a smaller, almost funny detail buried in the coverage. Apple's complaint states that OpenAI never responded when Apple's outside counsel reached out about the issue back in February. <a href="https://www.inc.com/esther-lian/apple-made-1-small-mistake-in-massive-lawsuit-against-openai/91374870" target="_blank">Inc.com reported</a> that emails reviewed by NBC News show OpenAI's counsel actually did respond, Apple's own lawyers mixed up two OpenAI employees with similar surnames and missed the reply sitting in their inbox. It's a minor point in a filing full of much bigger claims, but in a case built on "who knew what, when," a paperwork mixup on Apple's own side is the kind of detail defense counsel will enjoy pointing at.

## Why this is escalating, not settling down

Most corporate trade secrets disputes stay quiet: a cease-and-desist, a settlement, an NDA amendment, nobody outside the two companies hears about it. This one is doing the opposite.

On July 17, Apple sent legal preservation letters, formal notices requiring recipients to retain all potentially relevant documents and communications, to about 40 additional former employees now at OpenAI. These aren't lawsuits by themselves. They're the standard move a plaintiff makes when it believes the conduct named in a complaint might extend to people it hasn't sued yet, and wants to lock down evidence before anyone can plausibly claim they deleted something in the normal course of business.

The scale context matters here: over 400 former Apple employees currently work at OpenAI. Apple isn't alleging all 400 did anything wrong. But by widening the preservation net to 40 more names, and by describing its current findings as the "tip of the iceberg," Apple is signaling it thinks the pattern goes beyond Tan and Liu.

## What Apple is actually asking the court for

The complaint asks for both injunctive relief and damages:

- A preliminary and permanent injunction stopping OpenAI, io Products, and the named defendants from using, possessing, or disclosing the alleged trade secrets.
- Orders that could require OpenAI to isolate the affected design work, allow review of devices and code repositories, or demonstrate that its hardware development doesn't rely on Apple's information.
- Return of any Apple property and continued preservation of evidence.
- Compensatory damages, unjust-enrichment damages, a reasonable royalty, and attorneys' fees.

Apple hasn't put a number on the damages ask. Its filing says the eventual figure should reflect lost competitive advantage, the reduced value of the trade secrets now that they're allegedly exposed, and any R&D costs OpenAI avoided by allegedly using Apple's work instead of doing its own.

That last point is the real commercial stakes here. This isn't about money changing hands so much as it is about timing. OpenAI's hardware device, still widely expected in the second half of 2026, in screenless form factors reportedly codenamed "Sweetpea" and "Gumdrop," is a bet that OpenAI can compete with Apple on physical products, not just models. An injunction that forces OpenAI to rework parts of that hardware, or slows it down while the case plays out, is arguably worth more to Apple than any damages award.

## The developer read on this

Setting aside the legal drama, there's a mundane and very real lesson in here for anyone who's changed jobs in this industry: the "bring a sample of your old work to the interview" pattern that shows up throughout this complaint isn't unique to Apple and OpenAI. It's an extremely common interview practice across hardware and software companies, and it routinely walks right up to, or over, the line of what an NDA actually allows. If Apple's allegations hold up, "show and tell" style technical interviews are going to get a lot more legal scrutiny industry-wide, not just at these two companies.

The other detail worth internalizing: keeping a company laptop after you resign, and using it to pull files "just in case," is the kind of thing that feels harmless in the moment and reads very differently in a federal complaint. Discovery in cases like this doesn't just examine what you did, it examines your Slack messages, your calendar invites, and your badge logs from months before anyone thought a lawsuit was coming.

## What happens next

Trade secret cases like this typically take years, and most settle before trial once discovery makes the strength (or weakness) of each side's evidence clear to both parties. The near-term events to watch are procedural: whether OpenAI moves to dismiss any of the claims, whether Apple's request for a preliminary injunction gets a hearing date, and whether the list of preservation-letter recipients grows again.

The bigger picture is a genuinely new kind of rivalry. Apple and OpenAI aren't just competing on whose AI assistant is smarter, they're now direct competitors in consumer hardware, and Apple just used federal court to say it thinks OpenAI got there partly by taking a shortcut through Apple's own hallways.
