On September 25, 2025, Google's Safe Browsing system flagged the entire statichost.eu domain as deceptive. For about six hours, anyone in Chrome, Firefox, or Safari trying to reach the site, or any customer site hosted on it, got a full-page red warning instead of a page. The founder, Eric Selin, later wrote that the block reached roughly five billion devices worldwide. The actual cause was mundane: a batch of phishing pages had been thrown up on the free tier over a weekend, and Google's crawler blacklisted the whole domain rather than the offending subpaths. Selin found a "Request Review" button in Search Console, deleted the bad sites, and got an automated approval a few hours later. No human ever replied.

It's a small, specific story, and also the cleanest illustration of a much bigger one. Statichost.eu is a one-person static hosting company built explicitly as a non-American alternative to Vercel, Netlify, and Cloudflare Pages: European company, European datacenters, European ownership, no AWS or Cloudflare anywhere in the stack. And it spent six hours completely dependent on a review button inside a Google product to stay reachable at all. The pitch is sovereignty. The reality, for now, still runs through Mountain View.

## The pattern showing up across the stack

Statichost.eu isn't alone, and it isn't the top of the pyramid either. Over the past year, a similar move has shown up at pretty much every layer a small dev team touches:

- **Static hosting**: {{< link href="https://www.statichost.eu/" >}}statichost.eu{{< /link >}} - git-based deploys from GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket, Forgejo, SourceHut, or Azure DevOps, free SSL on custom domains, a CDN currently in private beta. Selin's stated goal, in his own words, is that "the internet got too complicated," with too much default routing through a handful of American vendors.
- **Git hosting**: {{< link href="https://pushin.eu" >}}pushin.eu{{< /link >}} - a GitHub-shaped alternative (issues, pull requests, CI runners, a GitHub-compatible REST API, a CLI called `pun`) hosted exclusively in French datacenters. It's explicit that the point is dodging the US CLOUD Act, and it promises it will never train AI models on hosted code. It's invite-only beta today, general availability targeted for early 2027, pricing "comparable to GitHub and GitLab."
- **DNS**: {{< link href="https://mullvad.net/en/blog/shutting-down-our-public-encrypted-dns-servers-and-sponsoring-quad9-instead" >}}Mullvad{{< /link >}} is shutting down its own public encrypted DNS resolvers by November 2, 2026, but not to retreat toward a US provider. It's redirecting that budget to sponsor {{< link href="https://quad9.net/" >}}Quad9{{< /link >}}, the Swiss non-profit resolver, calling it "the undisputed leader in the field" and running a privacy-focused public DNS service "a highly specialized undertaking" better left to a specialist than duplicated in-house.
- **Health data**: France's national Health Data Hub has been migrating off Microsoft Azure onto {{< link href="https://www.scaleway.com/" >}}Scaleway{{< /link >}}, the French cloud provider owned by Iliad.
- **Search**: French search engine Qwant partnered with the German non-profit Ecosia to launch Staan, a European-indexed search alternative, though Ecosia's roughly 20 million users are a rounding error next to Google's install base.

None of this reads like a coordinated plan. It reads like a lot of small teams independently arriving at the same conclusion at the same time, which is usually a better signal than one big company saying it.

## Why this is happening now, not five years ago

The sovereignty argument isn't new. What's new is that it has stopped being a compliance slide deck and started being cited as a reason to actually switch vendors, and there are three concrete reasons for that shift.

**The CLOUD Act keeps mattering more than people expect.** The 2018 US law lets American law enforcement compel a US-headquartered company to hand over data it controls, even when that data physically sits on a server in Frankfurt or Paris. This has been true for years, but it went from theoretical to operational as GDPR enforcement caught up with it: Austrian, French, and Italian data protection authorities have all issued rulings against US-based tools specifically over cross-border transfer violations, which is a much sharper prompt to migrate than a law review article.

**GDPR fines are compounding, not leveling off.** DLA Piper's January 2026 GDPR Fines and Data Breach Survey put the cumulative total since 2018 at roughly €7.1 billion. The CMS Enforcement Tracker, using a stricter counting method, has it closer to €6.1 billion across 2,685 cases. Either way, the trend line only goes up, and Ireland's Data Protection Commission alone accounts for about 57% of the total value, largely from cases against US platforms operating their EU business out of Dublin.

**The EU Data Act gave the threat of switching some teeth.** Since September 12, 2025, EU customers of cloud services have a legal right to switch providers, with a maximum 30-day transition window (extendable to seven months if it's technically infeasible faster) and no mandatory data-egress charge during the move. Full egress-fee elimination lands by January 12, 2027. Before this, "just switch clouds" was a sentence that ignored months of migration friction and surprise bandwidth bills. Now it's closer to true.

Layer a Gartner survey on top of that: in a poll of 241 Western European CIOs and IT leaders run in mid-2025, 61% said geopolitics will push them to increase reliance on local cloud providers, and 53% said geopolitics will actively restrict their use of global ones. Gartner's own prediction is that by 2030, more than three-quarters of enterprises outside the US will have a formal digital sovereignty strategy. Whether or not that forecast lands exactly, procurement teams are clearly already pricing this in.

## Brussels is doing the same thing, at a different scale

The indie projects aren't operating in a policy vacuum. The European Commission has been funding the exact same shift at the infrastructure layer. Its Sovereign Cloud tender awarded roughly €180 million (about $211 million) to four providers: Scaleway, Clever Cloud, OVHcloud, and STACKIT, the last one created by Schwarz Group, the parent company of the Lidl supermarket chain moving into cloud infrastructure. AWS submitted its own "European Sovereign Cloud" bid and was excluded. There's also open skepticism about S3NS, a joint venture between Google Cloud and the French defense contractor Thales that's meant to offer "sovereign" Google infrastructure. Critics point out that a joint venture with a US parent company doesn't obviously escape CLOUD Act exposure just because the paperwork says "sovereign."

Multiple national governments are also swapping out the software layer, not just the hosting layer: Austria, Denmark, Italy, and Germany have all announced moves off Windows and Microsoft Office toward Linux and LibreOffice in at least parts of their public sector. And the sentiment isn't purely institutional. After a wave of comments from the Trump administration about Greenland, apps explicitly marketed as European or as boycotts of American products briefly topped Denmark's App Store charts.

## The skepticism is earned, and it's worth reading

None of this means the indie alternatives are actually as good yet, and Hacker News did what Hacker News does when statichost.eu hit the front page: it read the fine print. Commenters compared the €9/month static hosting plan to a €5/month Scaleway VPS with unlimited bandwidth and asked what, exactly, the premium buys. Others found the site had no support for SSH public-key authentication, no multi-factor authentication, and (more concerning) unauthenticated rebuild-trigger endpoints that anyone with the URL could hit to kick off a redeploy. On the positive side, actual users reported being happy with the free tier and the direct, personal support that comes from emailing a founder instead of opening a ticket that a Fortune 500 support queue will get to eventually. FreeSewing, an open source sewing pattern generator with a real user base, migrated its site there.

That split is the honest state of the market right now: the European alternatives are real, they're growing, and they're not yet feature-complete replacements for the vendors they're competing with. It also isn't a clean story of Europe uniting against American cloud. Lufthansa and Air France both picked Starlink for in-flight WiFi despite the sovereignty push, and France's SNCF rail operator is reportedly considering the same. Sovereignty is a real preference, but it loses to "does the thing work reliably" often enough that nobody should assume it always wins.

## What this actually means if you're building something

If you're a developer or a small team deciding whether any of this changes what you should be running on, it comes down to five checks, not a philosophical position:

1. **Check who actually governs your data.** A server physically located in Frankfurt doesn't get you out of CLOUD Act exposure if the company running it is headquartered in the US. Ownership matters more than the datacenter's postal code.
2. **Map your actual GDPR exposure.** If you handle EU personal data professionally, look at what regulators in your specific sector have been fining lately, not just the aggregate €7.1 billion headline.
3. **Use the EU Data Act's switching rights as a real option, not a bluff.** The 30-day transition window and the ban on mandatory egress fees make "we'll just move" a credible plan in a way it wasn't three years ago.
4. **Compare features honestly, not badges.** A "European" host that's missing MFA or SSH key auth is a worse choice than a US host with proper security, for most projects. Sovereignty is one axis, not the whole scorecard.
5. **Price in operator concentration risk.** A one-person European hosting company can get accidentally blacklisted by Google, same as it can go down for a weekend when the founder is asleep. That's a different risk profile than a hyperscaler's five-nines SLA, not automatically a worse one, but a different one you should actually plan around.

The interesting part isn't that Europe wants to be less dependent on American infrastructure. Plenty of people have wanted that for a decade. It's that the tooling to actually do it, git hosting, static hosting, DNS, search, is now being built by small teams shipping real products instead of committees writing white papers, and that some of it is already good enough for real projects like FreeSewing to bet on. It's also, per the six-hour Google Safe Browsing outage, not done yet. Building your own cloud stack turns out to be a lot of small, unglamorous reliability work, and Europe's version of it is currently somewhere in the middle of that work, not at the end of it.
